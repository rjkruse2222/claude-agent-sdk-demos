# Restaurant Prime Cost: What It Is and How to Keep It Under 65%

In the restaurant industry, few numbers matter more than your prime cost. This single metric captures the bulk of your controllable expenses and serves as the most reliable indicator of operational efficiency. For restaurant owners and managers seeking profitability, understanding and managing prime cost isn't just important—it's essential for survival.

This guide explains everything you need to know about restaurant prime cost, from calculation methods to proven strategies for keeping it within profitable ranges.

## What Is Prime Cost?

Prime cost represents the sum of your two largest controllable expenses: cost of goods sold (COGS) and total labor costs. These expenses are considered "controllable" because management decisions directly influence them, unlike fixed costs such as rent or insurance.

The term "prime" reflects the primary importance of these costs in restaurant operations. Together, food and labor typically account for 55-65% of total restaurant revenue, making prime cost the most critical metric for evaluating operational efficiency.

### Components of Prime Cost

**Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** includes:
- Food purchases
- Beverage purchases (alcoholic and non-alcoholic)
- Paper goods and disposables
- Any items directly related to products sold

**Total Labor Costs** includes:
- Hourly wages (kitchen and front of house)
- Salaries (management and administrative)
- Payroll taxes (employer portion)
- Benefits (health insurance, retirement contributions)
- Workers' compensation insurance
- Bonuses and commissions

## The Prime Cost Formula

Calculating prime cost is straightforward:

**Prime Cost = Cost of Goods Sold + Total Labor Costs**

To express it as a percentage of sales:

**Prime Cost Percentage = (COGS + Total Labor) / Total Revenue × 100**

### Example Calculation

Consider a restaurant with these monthly figures:
- Food Cost: $40,000
- Beverage Cost: $12,000
- Total Labor (including taxes and benefits): $55,000
- Total Revenue: $180,000

Prime Cost = $40,000 + $12,000 + $55,000 = $107,000

Prime Cost Percentage = $107,000 / $180,000 × 100 = 59.4%

This restaurant is performing well, with prime cost under the 65% threshold.

## Why Prime Cost Matters More Than Individual Metrics

While tracking food cost and labor cost separately is important, prime cost provides a more complete picture for several reasons:

### Trade-offs Between Food and Labor

Restaurants often face trade-offs between food cost and labor cost. For example:

**Scratch Cooking vs. Prepared Products**: Making items from scratch typically lowers food costs but increases labor. Conversely, purchasing pre-prepared items raises food costs while reducing labor needs.

**Self-Service Elements**: Buffets or self-pour beverage stations reduce labor but may increase food waste and costs.

**Prep Work Intensity**: Complex dishes requiring extensive prep demand more labor hours.

Prime cost captures these trade-offs, showing the net impact of operational decisions on total controllable costs.

### Prevents Gaming Individual Metrics

Focusing solely on food cost might lead to decisions that increase labor cost by a larger amount—or vice versa. Prime cost prevents this siloed thinking by presenting the combined picture.

### Investor and Lender Relevance

Financial stakeholders examine prime cost to assess operational efficiency. A restaurant can have acceptable individual metrics but a problematic prime cost if both are at the high end of acceptable ranges.

## Industry Benchmarks by Restaurant Type

Target prime cost percentages vary by restaurant concept and operating model:

| Restaurant Type | Target Prime Cost |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Quick Service (QSR) | 55-60% |
| Fast Casual | 58-62% |
| Casual Dining | 60-65% |
| Fine Dining | 62-68% |
| Bars and Nightclubs | 50-55% |
| Catering Operations | 55-60% |

### Why Benchmarks Vary

**Service Style**: Full-service restaurants require more labor than counter-service concepts.

**Menu Complexity**: Complex menus demand more skilled (and expensive) labor and specialty ingredients.

**Alcohol Sales**: Beverage-heavy concepts benefit from higher margins on drinks, enabling higher food or labor percentages.

**Volume and Efficiency**: High-volume operations can spread labor costs over more sales, achieving lower prime cost percentages.

**Market Position**: Premium concepts can sustain higher prime costs because their pricing provides larger dollar margins.

### The 65% Rule

Industry convention holds that prime cost should not exceed 65% for most full-service restaurants. Beyond this level, remaining revenue is insufficient to cover rent, utilities, marketing, maintenance, and still generate acceptable profit.

For most concepts, targeting 60% or lower prime cost creates financial sustainability and room for unexpected expenses.

## Breaking Down Prime Cost Components

Understanding the typical breakdown of prime cost helps identify improvement opportunities.

### Food Cost

Food cost typically ranges from 28-35% of sales for most restaurants. Key factors influencing food cost include:

- Menu composition (protein-heavy vs. vegetable-focused)
- Ingredient sourcing (premium vs. standard)
- Waste management effectiveness
- Portion control consistency
- Supplier relationships and pricing

### Beverage Cost

Beverage costs vary significantly by type:
- Non-alcoholic beverages: 10-15%
- Beer: 20-25%
- Wine: 25-35%
- Liquor: 15-20%

Overall beverage cost for mixed programs typically runs 18-24%.

### Labor Cost

Labor cost benchmarks by restaurant type:
- Quick Service: 25-30%
- Fast Casual: 28-32%
- Casual Dining: 30-35%
- Fine Dining: 35-40%

Labor cost includes all payroll expenses, not just wages. Don't forget payroll taxes (7.65% employer FICA), benefits, and workers' compensation when calculating true labor cost.

## 8 Strategies to Reduce Prime Cost

Sustainable prime cost reduction requires systematic approaches rather than one-time cuts.

### 1. Optimize Scheduling to Match Demand

Labor scheduling has the most immediate impact on prime cost:

**Use Historical Data**: Analyze POS data to identify traffic patterns by day, daypart, and season. Schedule based on predicted demand rather than habit.

**Implement Staggered Shifts**: Start staff at different times based on when they're actually needed rather than having everyone arrive at once.

**Cross-Train Staff**: Employees who can work multiple positions provide scheduling flexibility and reduce the need for extra bodies during transitions.

**Monitor Overtime**: Overtime wages (1.5x) devastate labor cost. Track approaching overtime hours and adjust schedules proactively.

**Consider Split Shifts**: In markets where permissible, split shifts can staff peak periods without paying for slow times between.

### 2. Engineer Your Menu

Menu engineering simultaneously addresses food cost and revenue:

**Analyze Item Profitability**: Categorize items by contribution margin and popularity. Promote high-margin items and reconsider low performers.

**Balance Menu Mix**: Design menus so high-margin items balance lower-margin crowd-pleasers.

**Price Strategically**: Use psychological pricing and menu design to guide customers toward profitable choices.

**Simplify Offerings**: Fewer menu items means less inventory, less prep, and more efficiency.

**Update Regularly**: As costs change, update recipe costs and adjust prices or remove items as needed.

### 3. Control Food Waste

Food waste directly inflates food cost and prime cost:

**Track All Waste**: Implement a waste log capturing spoilage, prep waste, returns, and mistakes.

**Forecast Accurately**: Use sales data to predict demand and prep accordingly.

**Implement FIFO**: Ensure first-in-first-out rotation to minimize spoilage.

**Repurpose Trim**: Train chefs to use vegetable trim for stocks, meat trim for staff meals, and overripe produce for specials.

**Right-Size Portions**: Consistent portions prevent both waste and customer dissatisfaction.

### 4. Negotiate Supplier Contracts

Reducing ingredient costs directly lowers prime cost:

**Request Competitive Bids**: Solicit quotes from multiple suppliers annually.

**Join Purchasing Groups**: Group purchasing organizations (GPOs) provide volume pricing for smaller operators.

**Commit for Discounts**: Lock in prices or commit to volumes in exchange for better rates.

**Review Invoices Carefully**: Check every invoice against orders and contracted prices.

**Consider Alternative Products**: Be open to equivalent ingredients at lower costs.

### 5. Invest in Training

Well-trained staff improve both food cost and labor efficiency:

**Standard Recipes**: Ensure every dish is prepared consistently, eliminating costly variations.

**Portion Training**: Train cooks on exact portions using standardized tools.

**Efficiency Techniques**: Teach prep methods that maximize output per labor hour.

**Cross-Training**: Multi-skilled employees provide scheduling flexibility.

**Quality Standards**: Fewer mistakes mean less remakes and waste.

### 6. Leverage Technology

Modern technology reduces both COGS and labor:

**Inventory Software**: Automated tracking, par levels, and purchasing recommendations reduce waste and stockouts.

**Scheduling Software**: Labor optimization tools create efficient schedules while ensuring compliance.

**POS Analytics**: Sales data informs forecasting, menu decisions, and labor planning.

**Kitchen Display Systems**: Eliminate paper tickets, reduce errors, and speed production.

**Reservations and Waitlist Tools**: Better planning enables staffing optimization.

### 7. Review Benefits Structure

Benefits significantly impact total labor cost:

**Benchmark Plans**: Ensure your benefit offerings are competitive but not excessive for your market.

**Consider Alternatives**: High-deductible plans with HSA contributions may cost less while still providing value.

**Manage Workers' Comp**: Implement safety programs to reduce claims and premiums.

**Review Annually**: Benefits costs change; review options every renewal period.

### 8. Analyze and Adjust Continuously

Sustainable prime cost management requires ongoing attention:

**Weekly Calculations**: Calculate prime cost weekly rather than waiting for monthly close.

**Variance Investigation**: When prime cost exceeds targets, identify the specific cause.

**Trend Monitoring**: Track prime cost over time to identify gradual creep.

**Benchmarking**: Compare your performance to industry standards and similar concepts.

## Balancing Food Cost and Labor Cost

The most effective prime cost management recognizes the interconnection between food and labor costs.

### When Higher Food Cost Makes Sense

Certain situations justify higher food cost:

**Pre-Prepared Products**: Buying pre-cut vegetables, marinated proteins, or assembled components raises food cost but can dramatically reduce labor needs and skill requirements.

**Premium Ingredients**: Higher-quality ingredients may command prices that more than offset their cost, improving overall profitability despite higher food cost percentage.

**Speed of Service**: Items enabling faster service can increase table turns and total revenue, diluting labor cost percentage.

### When Higher Labor Cost Makes Sense

Similarly, higher labor investment sometimes pays off:

**Scratch Cooking**: Making items from scratch typically lowers food cost and can differentiate your restaurant, supporting premium pricing.

**Skilled Staff**: Better-trained staff make fewer mistakes, waste less product, and require less supervision.

**Customer Experience**: Service-intensive concepts can command higher prices that absorb labor costs.

### Finding Your Balance

The optimal balance depends on your concept, market, and competitive positioning. Test changes carefully, measuring the net impact on prime cost rather than individual components.

## Tracking Prime Cost Effectively

Accurate tracking enables informed decision-making.

### Establish Tracking Systems

**Integrate POS and Accounting**: Ensure sales data flows to your accounting system automatically.

**Track Labor in Real-Time**: Use time clocks integrated with scheduling software.

**Calculate COGS Properly**: Include all product costs, not just food.

**Include All Labor Costs**: Don't forget payroll taxes and benefits.

### Recommended Tracking Frequency

**Daily**: Monitor labor cost daily through timekeeping systems.

**Weekly**: Calculate prime cost weekly for timely intervention capability.

**Monthly**: Detailed analysis for trends and strategic decisions.

**Quarterly**: Comprehensive review comparing to goals and benchmarks.

### Create Useful Reports

Effective prime cost reporting includes:
- Current period prime cost percentage
- Comparison to target/budget
- Comparison to prior periods
- Breakdown by food cost vs. labor cost
- Trend visualization
- Variance explanation

## Key Takeaways

Prime cost management is the cornerstone of restaurant profitability. Remember these essential points:

1. **Prime cost combines COGS and labor**, your two largest controllable expenses.

2. **Target 65% or below** for most full-service restaurants, with lower targets for QSR and bar concepts.

3. **Consider trade-offs** between food cost and labor cost when making operational decisions.

4. **Manage both components** through scheduling optimization, menu engineering, waste reduction, and supplier negotiation.

5. **Invest in training and technology** to improve efficiency in both areas.

6. **Track weekly** to catch issues before they become expensive problems.

7. **Analyze continuously** to identify improvement opportunities and maintain performance.

By keeping your prime cost under control, you create the financial foundation necessary for profitability, growth, and long-term success in the competitive restaurant industry.
